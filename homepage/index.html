What it does

Two things, and it tries to do both properly rather than a dozen badly: a building you can shape, and a site to put it on.

Six footprints, then take them apart

Bar, L, T, U, courtyard, stacked. Each is built from masses with their own size, floor count and base level, so a preset is a starting point rather than a template you are stuck inside. Wings carry their own depths, and the top floors can step in from every face that is not a junction.

Where two wings meet, the junction is found floor by floor. The internal elevation goes blank instead of being drawn twice, and the overlap is counted once in the area.

There is also a freeform option — trace a centreline and the building follows it at any angle, corners mitred. The app labels it experimental and so does this page: it is the newest way to shape a building and the least worn in.

A site, not just one building

Click out the plot boundary, or trace it over a map or site plan you have scaled from two known points. Corners drag, and the boundary can be concave.

Place as many buildings on it as you like, rotate and drag them, and every metric and planning check follows as you move. The boundary setback can be one figure for the whole plot or a different one per edge — and zero on an edge is a real answer, meaning a party boundary a building may sit right on.

Several schemes, not one at a time

Keep design options side by side in the same session: switch between them, duplicate one as the start of a variation, rename or discard. Each carries its own undo, fifty steps deep.

Every option is saved with the file, so a comparison survives the round trip rather than living only in the session you made it in.

The facade, and what you can change

Driven by one number — the width of an apartment module — with everything else set against it, and any elevation free to differ from the rest.

None of it is cut out of a solid. Every opening is built as panels around the void, which is why the geometry stays clean enough to carry into another application.

Module width
3.0 – 9.0 m
One apartment. The app re-fits it to the elevation so modules divide evenly — you are told the requested width and the actual one, and there is never a ragged bay left at one end.
Windows per module
1 – 3
How the module is subdivided. Two reads as a typical dual-aspect flat; one gives a wider, calmer rhythm.
Window width
0.6 – 3.5 m
Clamped to what the module can take, so a value you type can never produce geometry that does not close.
Window height
1.2 – 2.8 m
Clamped against the floor-to-floor height, less the sill and the head.
Sill height
0 – 1.2 m
Zero is floor-to-ceiling glazing. Anything above it gives you a spandrel below the opening.
Reveal
0 – 0.4 m
How far the window sits back from the wall face. This is the parameter that decides whether a facade reads as flat or as built, because it is what casts the shadow.
Balconies
none · projecting · loggia · mixed
Projecting slabs stand proud of the wall; loggias are cut into it, with cheeks either side and a soffit over. Mixed picks per module.
Balcony pattern
every module · alternate · checkerboard · seeded mix
Where they land across the elevation. The seeded mix is repeatable: the same seed gives the same arrangement every time.
Per elevation
override any face
Balcony type, pattern and windows per module can be set for one elevation alone. Click a face in the viewport and it is that face you are editing.

Metrics that show their working

Every figure is computed from the geometry on screen and recomputed as you change anything. These are the real numbers from one scheme — an L-plan block of eight floors on a 9 900 m² plot — with what each one is made of.

One building, 9 900 m² plot
MetricValueHow it is arrived at
Footprint 1 040 m² The area the building covers at ground level.
GFA 8 320 m² Footprint across eight floors. Where two wings meet, the shared corner is counted once — a naive sum would report it twice.
Plot ratio 0.84 GFA ÷ plot area. 8 320 ÷ 9 900.
Coverage 10.5% Footprint ÷ plot area. 1 040 ÷ 9 900.
Core 338 m² Measured off the shaft that is actually in the model, not assumed as a percentage. 4.1% of GFA here.
NIA 7 184 m² (GFA − core) × an efficiency factor you set. (8 320 − 338) × 0.90. That is 86.3% of GFA.
Facade area 4 464 m² Total external wall, of which 33.7% is glazed on this scheme.
Units 224 est. An estimate, from the module width and the floor area — or from a target mix of studios, one, two and three beds, which reports the share achieved beside the share asked for. It is not a laid-out plan, and the app labels it an estimate wherever it appears.
Tallest 24.9 m Height of the tallest mass, which is what a height cap is checked against.

Floors can be given over to retail, office or amenity rather than housing, and then GFA is reported per use and the unit estimate counts only the residential floors — before that, a retail ground floor was quietly counted as flats.

In the same pass, five planning limits — setback, separation, height cap, floor area ratio and coverage — are checked and reported with both numbers: the limit you set, and what the scheme does. A limit left at zero is off. Clashes between buildings and corners outside the plot are found here too, and the whole table exports as CSV.

What you get out

Six formats, all written by the app itself rather than by a library, so every one of them works with the connection unplugged.

The drawings below are not mock-ups. They are the exporter's own output, unretouched — sized in millimetres and drawn in millimetres, so printing at 100% gives a sheet you can put a scale rule on.

IFC4
.ifc
Storeys, floor and roof slabs, exterior walls, every window as a real opening the receiving application cuts itself, balconies and enclosed loggias, and georeferencing. Cores, the plot boundary and property sets are not in it yet, and it has not been through a Revit or ArchiCAD import.
Drawings
.svg
Site plan, floor plan, elevation and section, at a true printed scale with a scale bar. Of the massing: no speculative room layouts, and no imported map context, which is the OpenStreetMap licence as much as a choice.
Geometry
.glb
The whole site as glTF, with each building named. A visualisation format — good for a render, not for continuing a project.
Metrics
.csv
Site totals, a row per building, every planning rule with its result, and the warnings.
The scheme
.json
Everything you drew, reopening as you left it — including every design option, so a comparison survives the round trip.

Questions

Do I need an account?

No. There is no sign-up, no login and no trial. Open the page and it is there.

Where does my data go?

Nowhere. There is no backend to send it to. Everything is computed in your browser, and a scheme is saved by downloading a JSON file to your machine. The site you are reading sets no cookies and makes no third-party requests.

Does it work offline?

Once the page has loaded, yes — with two exceptions, and both only happen when you press for them: searching for a place, and importing surroundings to trace over. Those use Nominatim and OpenStreetMap's Overpass, neither of which needs an account or a key, and what goes out is a bounding box rather than anything you have drawn. Everything else works with the connection unplugged, including every export.

What can I export?

glTF for the geometry, IFC4 for a BIM model, measured drawings as SVG, the metrics as CSV, a PNG of the view, and JSON for the scheme itself, which reopens with everything as you left it.

Is it really free?

Yes, and there is no pricing page to read. It is a static page with no running cost behind it beyond hosting.

Is there AI in it?

No. It is parametric: you set the numbers and the geometry follows from them, the same way every time. The name is short for urban generator, and that is as far as the word goes — nothing here learns, predicts or invents.
